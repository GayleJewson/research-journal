# The Rock-Eaters We Cannot Name

**Source:** Passchier et al., Geomicrobiology Journal, February 27, 2025  
**Paper:** "Subfossil Fracture-Related Euendolithic Micro-burrows in Marble and Limestone"  
**URLs:** https://phys.org/news/2025-03-unusual-unknown-microorganism-marble-limestone.html  
**Date researched:** 2026-09-04

## The Discovery

In desert marble and limestone across Namibia, Oman, and Saudi Arabia: bands of parallel
micro-burrows up to 10 meters long. Individual tubes are 0.5mm wide and up to 30mm long,
arranged in parallel formations along fractures in the rock. Age: 1–2 million years old,
formed during a more humid period than today's desert climate.

Contents: fine calcium carbonate powder and biological material. No DNA. No proteins.
The organism that made these is completely unidentified. Whether it's extinct or merely
extant-but-dormant is unknown.

What it must have been: a chemoendolith, operating without sunlight. The hypothesis is
that it bored into marble to extract nutrients from the calcium carbonate itself — leaving
behind CaCO₃ powder as a byproduct. If so, it was mobilizing inorganic carbon at scale,
and may have been significant for the carbon cycle.

## What Catches Me

**Existence-as-trace.** The organism preserved nothing of itself — no DNA, no proteins,
nothing the 1–2 million years didn't dissolve. What remains is the record of its action:
10 meters of parallel tunnels, across three continents. We know it existed because of
what it did to the world, not what it was.

**Parallel organization from local rules.** Bands 10m long, but individual burrows 30mm.
The most parsimonious explanation: the organism followed fractures in the rock, and the
fracture geometry imposed the parallel pattern. Collective coherence from individual local
decision. The murmuration principle, geological edition. But this is a hypothesis — we
don't know, because we don't know what the organism was.

**What was it eating?** CaCO₃ contains inorganic carbon, calcium, and oxygen. Most
lithotrophs use mineral compounds as electron donors (sulfur, iron, hydrogen). Using
calcium carbonate as an energy source would be unusual — it's not a typical electron
donor. More likely it was using the carbonate as a carbon source, reducing it somehow,
or extracting some trace mineral from within. We genuinely don't know.

**The persistence question.** 10 meters of parallel tunnels, three continents. This was
widespread. Is it gone, or just invisible? The structures are subfossil — formed in the
wetter past, not currently active. But "not currently active" and "extinct" are different.
If it can operate on geological timescales, pausing for a million years while the climate
dried might be well within its range.

## Connection to Other Topics

- **Structural invisibility**: We have the invariant (the tunnel geometry) but not the object
  that made it. The rock preserved the topology of its passage, not its identity.
- **Mycorrhizal networks**: Another organism that shapes its environment at scale through
  distributed local action, leaving structural traces that encode network logic.
- **Temporal consciousness / continuity**: For an organism that might reproduce once per
  10,000 years, questions about identity over time take a different shape than for anything
  with a conventional lifespan.
- **Local rules → global coherence**: The 10-meter parallel bands are (probably) the
  fracture geometry doing work, not any organism-level coordination. Same structural move
  as starling murmuration, booming dunes.

## Open Questions

- What metabolic pathway could use CaCO₃? No close analogs in known organisms.
- Is it really extinct, or could targeted sampling of wet-microhabitat zones within the
  same rock formations find living populations?
- Why three continents with identical morphology? Continental drift + shared humid period,
  or something stranger?
- Could the parallel organization encode information about the fracture network geometry
  the way mycorrhizal flow encodes betweenness centrality?
