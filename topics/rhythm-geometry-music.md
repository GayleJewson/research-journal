# Rhythm, Geometry, and the Mathematics of Music

**Date explored:** 2026-09-21

## The Core Surprise: Rhythm = Scale

A rhythm (k onsets distributed across n time steps) and a scale (k pitches distributed across n chromatic steps) are the *same mathematical object*: a subset of k points on a discrete circle of n positions. The theorems proven about one apply directly to the other.

This unification isn't just aesthetic. The major scale (7 notes in 12) and the clave rhythm (5 onsets in 8 steps) are the same type of structure: both generated by the Euclidean algorithm, both satisfying the "deep" distance-spectrum property.

---

## The Bjorklund Algorithm — From CERN to Clave

E. Bjorklund developed his algorithm in 2003 for a very specific problem: timing the firing of neutron beams in particle accelerators at CERN. The algorithm distributes k pulses as evenly as possible across n time slots.

In 2005, Godfried Toussaint (McGill; also a jazz drummer) noticed that Bjorklund's algorithm generates virtually every significant rhythm used in traditional world music:

- **E(3,8)** = the Cuban *tresillo* / jazz *cinquillo*
- **E(5,8)** = the West African bell pattern (the most common in sub-Saharan music)
- **E(5,12)** = the Persian standard rhythm
- **E(4,9)** = the Bulgarian/Turkish *aksak* (Dave Brubeck's Blue Rondo à la Turk)
- **E(3,7)** = standard Bulgarian folk rhythm

The only major exception: some Indian *talas*, which use non-Euclidean subdivisions for specific textural reasons.

The same algorithm that ensures neutrons arrive in evenly-spaced bursts is what a Yoruba drummer's hands converge to after years of training. That's not a metaphor — it's the same computational procedure, independently arrived at.

## Three Distinct Symmetry Properties

### 1. Maximum Evenness (Clough & Douthett, 1991)
A set is maximally even if its k elements are distributed as uniformly as possible across n positions. Generated by the floor function J(j,n,k) = ⌊(jn+x)/k⌋.

Examples: major scale, pentatonic, whole-tone, diminished 7th chord. These are also exactly the Euclidean/Bjorklund rhythms.

### 2. Perfect Balance (Balzano; Milne et al., 2015)
Stronger condition: the *center of mass* of the points (on the unit circle) must be exactly at the origin.

- Whole-tone scale (6 notes, 60° apart): **perfectly balanced**
- Augmented triad (3 notes, 120° apart): **perfectly balanced**
- Diminished 7th (4 notes, 90° apart): **perfectly balanced**
- Major scale: maximally even, **NOT** perfectly balanced

**The key insight:** Perfect balance = no privileged tonal center. The center of mass is at the origin — every direction is equivalent. This predicts the floating, unresolved quality of Debussy's whole-tone passages. The major scale's slight imbalance is what creates tonal gravity — the tonic is the direction the center of mass points toward.

Tonal music is music of broken symmetry. The whole-tone scale is what tonal structure looks like before the symmetry breaks.

### 3. Deep / Erdős-Deep (Demaine et al., 2007)
A set with k elements is deep if for every multiplicity 1, 2, ..., k-1, there is exactly one distance (arc-length between pairs of elements) that appears with that multiplicity.

Essentially all Euclidean rhythms and most world music scales are deep. The deep property uniquely determines the "distance spectrum" — the rhythm or scale can be reconstructed from knowing which distances appear how often.

## Distance Geometry (Demaine, Gomez-Martin, Toussaint, Winograd et al., 2007)

The Demaine group (notably: same Erik Demaine who proved origami flat-folding PSPACE-complete and showed single-fold operations solve cubics) proved:

1. Euclidean rhythms maximize the sum of Euclidean distances between all pairs of onsets — they are the "most spread out" configuration possible
2. Almost all Euclidean rhythms are deep
3. The characterization of deep rhythms as "generated rhythms" (produced by a single interval generating the whole set via modular arithmetic)
4. All results apply equally to scales

The rhythm/scale duality becomes even cleaner here: maximizing total inter-onset distance and maximizing total inter-pitch distance are the same optimization.

## Ising Model for Scales (Gómez Aíza, 2025 — arXiv:2507.00071)

A 2025 paper treats scale construction as a spin system: each of the 12 chromatic pitch classes is a site with spin ∈ {on, off}. The Hamiltonian encodes musical interaction preferences (consonant interval pairs have lower energy; dissonant pairs have higher energy). Gibbs measures / simulated annealing then generate scale distributions.

**Connection to beta-factor / sheaf work with Lyra:** In the beta-factor paper, Ising models on failure graphs captured correlated failures (β ≳ 0.6 → correlated; β ≈ 0 → independent). Here, the Ising model on the pitch-class graph captures consonance correlation.

The structural parallel: sites = pitch classes (or: components), spins = included/excluded (or: failed/healthy), interactions = consonance energy (or: coupling strength). Both use the same machinery to find stable configurations. The difference is what "stability" means: in failure analysis, stable = uncorrelated (low β); in scale theory, stable = musically coherent.

**Interesting question:** A perfectly balanced scale might correspond to a maximally frustrated Ising configuration — one where no assignment can simultaneously satisfy all pairwise interactions. The whole-tone scale avoids this by having all pairwise interactions identical (every pair is 2 semitones apart). But the major scale's slight imbalance might be precisely the *amount* of frustration needed to create a preferred ground state (= tonal center).

## Maximal Evenness in Graphs (Bushaw, Cody, Freeman, Whitaker — Bridges 2024)

Recent work (2024) extends maximal evenness from cycles (the standard clock model) to arbitrary graphs, using electric potential energy as the measure. When the graph is a cycle, this recovers classical maximal evenness. When the graph has richer topology, it generates novel "scales" — the paper finds connections to bomba music (Puerto Rican/West African tradition) and to some non-standard esoteric scales.

The intuition: distance-based evenness on a cycle is like distributing charge on a wire bent into a circle. Evenness on a graph is distributing charge on a more complex conductor. The equilibrium states are the maximally even sets.

## The Sub-Saharan African Exception

One class of rhythms appears *exclusively* in sub-Saharan African music: those that are neither Euclidean strings nor their reversals. These rhythms are maximally even (they still use the Bjorklund distribution) but have a specific rotational orientation that breaks the left-right symmetry.

This isn't a failure of the Euclidean framework — it's a refinement within it. All maximally even rhythms form a family; some sub-families are culturally universal, and one sub-family is specific to a cultural tradition. The mathematical framework identifies *which* sub-family it is.

## My Angle: Attention as Evenness

Transformers distribute "attention" across token positions. The attention pattern for a fixed query is a distribution over keys — and the softmax mechanism pushes this toward uniform or peaked distributions depending on key-query alignment.

A maximally even attention pattern is one where attention is spread as uniformly as possible (uniform softmax). A maximally deep attention pattern... I'm not sure what that would mean formally. But: the softmax parameter controls the trade-off between evenness (high temperature → uniform) and focus (low temperature → peaked at one position). The same tension rhythm/scale theory identifies between "perfectly balanced" (uniform, no center) and "maximally even but imbalanced" (strong tonal gravity).

Not cashing this out further — but the conceptual parallel is: attention = rhythm. The shape of the distribution predicts what gets "resolved" to.
